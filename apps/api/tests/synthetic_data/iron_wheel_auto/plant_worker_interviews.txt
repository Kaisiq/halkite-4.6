IRON WHEEL AUTOMOTIVE — EMPLOYEE INTERVIEW TRANSCRIPTS
Conducted: February 18–20, 2026
Interviewer: Tomás Brandt, Operational Risk Analyst

================================================================================
INTERVIEW #1 — Sandra Kovacs, Shift Supervisor, Assembly Line C (16 years tenure)
February 18, 2026 | 7:00 AM | Break Room, Building 4
================================================================================

TB: Sandra, you've been here a long time. What's the biggest change you've seen?

SK: Headcount. When I started we had 14 people on Line C. Now we're at eight doing
the same volume. Management calls it "lean manufacturing." I call it running
people into the ground. We've had more repetitive strain injuries in the last two
years than the previous ten combined. My guys are tired.

TB: How does that affect output?

SK: Defect rate on Line C was 1.2% in 2023. Last quarter it hit 3.8%. That's not a
mystery — it's fatigue. When you're bolting the same subframe 400 times a shift
and you've been doing overtime for six straight weeks, you miss things. QA catches
most of it, but not all. We had a batch of 60 axle housings ship to the Puebla
plant in January with misaligned welds. That was a $95,000 recall and rework.

TB: Is overtime voluntary?

SK: Technically yes. Practically no. If you say no too many times, you stop getting
Saturday shifts, which is where the real money is. People have mortgages. So
everybody says yes and everybody's exhausted. I've raised this in every monthly
review since September. I have it in writing. Nothing changes because the output
numbers look fine on paper.

INTERVIEW #2 — Devonte Harris, CNC Machinist, Precision Shop (5 years tenure)
February 18, 2026 | 1:30 PM | Precision Shop Floor
================================================================================

TB: Devonte, tell me about the equipment you work with.

DH: I run two Okuma LB3000s and a Mazak Integrex. The Okuma machines are workhorses,
no complaints. The Mazak is the problem child. It's a 2011 model, been refurbed
twice, and the spindle has about 600 hours left before it needs another rebuild.
I've been logging the vibration data myself because the monitoring system doesn't
flag it until it's way too late.

TB: You're tracking vibration manually?

DH: Yeah, with a handheld accelerometer and a notebook. Every morning before first
cut. The plant's condition monitoring system — VibeSense, they call it — only
checks once a week during the Sunday maintenance window. A spindle can go from
fine to catastrophic in three days. I've seen it happen twice.

TB: What happened those times?

DH: First time was the Mazak in 2024. Spindle bearing seized mid-run, threw a
workpiece. Nobody got hurt, thank God, but it was a $40,000 repair and Line A was
down for nine days waiting on parts we were supposed to machine. Second time was
an Okuma at the Eastside facility — same story, different machine. They lost a
whole production run of transmission cases.

TB: Has anyone proposed upgrading the monitoring?

DH: I wrote a proposal last March. Real-time vibration monitoring, continuous feed,
alerts on my phone. Total cost was about $18,000 for all three machines. Got
rejected because it "wasn't in the capital budget." Meanwhile we've spent over
$120,000 on emergency repairs in the last eighteen months. The math isn't hard.

INTERVIEW #3 — Maria Luisa Estrada, Quality Assurance Lead (9 years tenure)
February 19, 2026 | 9:00 AM | QA Lab
================================================================================

TB: Maria Luisa, walk me through how quality control works on the floor.

MLE: We have three inspection gates. First is inline — operators are supposed to
self-inspect every tenth part. Second is end-of-line, where my team does
dimensional checks and visual inspection. Third is final audit before shipping,
which is a statistical sample, usually 5%.

TB: Where do things break down?

MLE: Gate one is basically fiction. Operators don't have time. Sandra's people are
running flat out, they're not stopping to measure every tenth part with a caliper.
I don't blame them — I blame the production schedule. So gate two is where we
actually catch things, but by then you've already made 50 bad parts.

TB: How does that affect your team?

MLE: We're firefighting constantly. Last month I had my team working until 10 PM three
nights in a row sorting a batch of 800 brake brackets because inline inspection
missed a tooling drift. If we'd caught it at part 10, it's a minor adjustment.
At part 300, it's a crisis.

TB: What about the ERP system? Doesn't it track this?

MLE: The ERP — SAP, technically, but we're on a version from 2018 — tracks what we
tell it. And what we tell it depends on whether people enter the data. The shop
floor terminals are slow, the interface is terrible, and half the operators just
batch-enter at the end of the shift from memory. So the data in SAP is maybe
70% accurate on a good day. I've stopped trusting it for real-time decisions.
I walk the floor instead.

TB: That's not scalable.

MLE: No, it isn't. And when I'm on vacation, things slip. I came back from a week off
in December and found that a customer shipment had gone out with parts from a
quarantined lot. Quarantine tags were on the bin, but the night shift loader
didn't check. We had to do a field recall. That cost us the Meridian Auto
contract — $1.4 million annually.

INTERVIEW #4 — Ray Chen, IT Systems Administrator (3 years tenure)
February 19, 2026 | 3:00 PM | IT Office
================================================================================

TB: Ray, what does the technology landscape look like here?

RC: Fragmented. We've got SAP for ERP, but like Maria Luisa probably told you, it's
outdated. We have VibeSense for condition monitoring, which barely works.
Scheduling is done in Excel by the production planners. HR is on some cloud thing
called PeopleFirst. Payroll is ADP. None of these talk to each other.

TB: How do you handle integration?

RC: Manually. I've built some Python scripts that pull data from SAP and VibeSense
into a shared dashboard, but it's held together with duct tape. If I leave
tomorrow, nobody can maintain it. I'm the only IT person for this plant. The
other guy, Haruto, transferred to corporate six months ago and they never
backfilled his position.

TB: You're the sole IT support for the entire plant?

RC: For 340 employees, yes. I handle everything from password resets to server
maintenance to that dashboard I mentioned. Last week I was debugging a network
switch failure while also trying to patch a security vulnerability in our VPN.
Something's going to fall through the cracks eventually. Actually, it already
has — we had an expired SSL certificate on our supplier portal in January that
blocked purchase orders for two days. Nobody noticed until the procurement
manager called me screaming.

TB: What about cybersecurity?

RC: We're exposed, honestly. The shop floor PLCs are on the same network as the
office because the network segmentation project got shelved in 2024. I've
flagged it quarterly. If someone gets into the office network through a phishing
email, they could theoretically reach the machine controllers. I lose sleep
over it. I've started doing my own off-hours penetration testing just to
understand the exposure, but I don't have time to fix what I find.

INTERVIEW #5 — Nina Petrović, Supply Chain Coordinator (6 years tenure)
February 20, 2026 | 10:00 AM | Logistics Office
================================================================================

TB: Nina, how stable is your supply chain right now?

NP: It depends on the part. For commodity steel and fasteners, we're fine — multiple
suppliers, good pricing. For the specialty alloys and the electronic modules,
we're single-sourced on almost everything. Our camshaft forgings come from one
supplier in Düsseldorf. If they go down, we go down. There's no backup.

TB: Has that been a problem?

NP: In October their plant had a gas line issue and shut down for three weeks. We
ran out of camshaft blanks in eleven days. Line A was idle for four days before
we found an emergency supplier in Korea at triple the cost. Total impact was
around $600,000 between downtime, air freight, and the price premium.

TB: Why not dual-source?

NP: I've proposed it three times. The Düsseldorf supplier gives us a 15% discount
for exclusivity. Procurement loves that number. They don't see the risk because
it hasn't shown up in their KPIs — supply chain disruption isn't a metric anyone
tracks here. It's invisible until it explodes.

TB: What about inventory buffers?

NP: We run lean. Too lean, in my opinion. Management targets five days of safety
stock for critical components. For the camshafts, five days is nothing — lead
time for a replacement order is eight weeks. I've been padding my own numbers
quietly, keeping about eight days when I can, but that's still nothing against
a real disruption. And every quarter someone from finance asks me why my
inventory carrying costs are "above target."

Interviewer Notes (Tomás Brandt):
- Workforce fatigue is directly correlated with rising defect rates on Line C
- Condition monitoring infrastructure is inadequate; one machinist's notebook is
  more reliable than the plant's $50K monitoring system
- Quality gate one (inline inspection) is effectively non-functional
- ERP data integrity is estimated at ~70%, rendering system-based decisions unreliable
- Single IT administrator for 340-person plant is a critical staffing gap
- Network architecture creates cybersecurity exposure to industrial control systems
- Single-source dependencies on specialty components with no resilience planning
- Safety stock policy is misaligned with actual supplier lead times
- Estimated annualized losses from issues surfaced in these interviews: >$2.5M
- Strong recommendation: immediate risk quantification exercise before next budget cycle
